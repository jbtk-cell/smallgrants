Everything here comes from filings foundations submitted to the IRS. This page says where the numbers come from, what the data cannot tell you, and which of the original ideas failed when it was tested.

Where the data comes from

Every private foundation in the United States files a Form 990-PF each year. Part XV of that form lists the grants it paid out: the recipient, their city and state, a stated purpose, and the amount. The IRS publishes these filings as bulk XML, roughly 27 GB across 49 archives for filing years 2022 through 2026.

That is the entire source. Nothing here was submitted by a foundation, bought from a data vendor, or chosen by an editor. If a foundation appears, it is because it filed, and the figures shown are the ones it reported.

Five of the 49 archives use a compression format that most tools report as a corrupt file. Those five hold 89,345 filings and about 1,044,450 grants. Believing the error message would have dropped an eighth of the corpus without any sign that something was missing.

What the record does not identify

Foundations write recipients as free text with no EIN. A filing says CONNECTICUT FOREST & PARK ASSOC and stops there, so connecting a grant to a known organization means matching on the name. Names are matched against the IRS Business Master File, which lists about 1.98 million registered organizations.

47.0% matched on name and state  ·  3.1% organizations the filer typed into the person field  ·  9.7% grants to individuals, which are not in the register by design  ·  40.2% unresolved

Those unresolved grants are real money to real organizations. They fail to match because they were written under a trading name, an abbreviation, or a misspelling. This is the biggest known weakness in the product and it has a consequence worth stating plainly: when a foundation's history looks thin here, that might mean it gives narrowly, or it might mean its grantees sit in the unmatched 40%.

One matching rule was removed after it was tested. Where a name was unique across the whole country, the system used to accept the match without checking the state. That produced 3.3% of all matches, and 97.6% of them were wrong. A well known charity files under a decorated legal name while some small unrelated organization holds the plain one, so PLANNED PARENTHOOD resolved to a single San Antonio organization across 5,175 grants in 46 states. National uniqueness turned out to measure obscurity rather than identity.

The idea that did not survive testing

The original design rested on something called the openness ratio: the share of a foundation's grantees each year that it had never funded before. The reasoning was that a foundation constantly taking on new grantees is more likely to consider a stranger.

It can be checked, because roughly 70% of foundations state on Part XV line 2 whether they accept unsolicited requests. Their own answer is the truth to test against. The ratio is only a guess at it.

The gap runs in the direction the idea needs, and at this sample size it is not chance (Welch t = 33.6). It is also small: Cohen's d = 0.286. The number that settled the argument is in the first row. Foundations that state in writing that they accept no unsolicited requests still replace about a third of their grantees every year. Turnover does not mean reachable.

So the ratio dropped from 10% of the ranking weight to 5%, and it is marked a weak signal wherever it appears. It was not thrown out, because it does carry information. It is not relied on, because it does not carry much.

An earlier run of the same test reported a larger effect, d = 0.355. That number was wrong. It counted grants to individuals, which turned the ratio into a scholarship detector, since a fund paying a fresh cohort of students each year scores near 1.0 by construction.

Cause carries the most weight and is read from grant history rather than from any mission statement. 37.7% of stated purposes are boilerplate, things like GENERAL OPERATING or CHARITABLE, so where the purpose text says nothing the recipient's own registered activity code stands in for it.

The strongest filter is not a score at all. It is Part XV line 2, where 70.3% of foundations state that they fund only organizations they select themselves. Those foundations are hidden unless you ask for them. That is a fact each one filed about itself, not a guess made about it from the outside.

What this cannot tell you

  • Whether a foundation will say yes. Nothing here predicts that.
  • Whether it is still giving. Filings lag the tax year, often by more than a year, and every foundation shows the most recent year on record.
  • Anything about public charities that make grants. They report on Form 990 Schedule I, which is not in this corpus. Only private foundations file a 990-PF.
  • Whether the unmatched 40% would change the picture for any particular foundation. It might.

Where the record is too thin to support a claim, the review says so rather than guessing. A foundation with no grant to an individual on file and most of its recipients unidentified is reported that way, with the unidentified share stated, instead of being turned into a confident no.

Checking it

The source is public. The archives are at apps.irs.gov/pub/epostcard/990/xml/ and the Business Master File is published alongside them. Every figure on this page came from a query against the corpus built from those files, and the build runs end to end from the raw archives with no manual steps in the middle.

The full record of what was measured, including the tests that failed and the defects found while checking, is kept with the code rather than summarized here.
